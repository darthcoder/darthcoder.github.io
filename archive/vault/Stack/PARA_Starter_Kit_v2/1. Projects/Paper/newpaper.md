## A novel method for Factorisation

A novel method for factorization is presented which is based on the observation that the multiplication of two numbers can be thought of as a convolution operation on the series of the digits of the two numbers. This process can then be run in reverse as a sort of a back-propagation to try and guess the two digits which when multiplied lead to the corresponding digit of the number we are trying to factorize. Naively, it might appear that there are $2\times 10^n$ possibilities for the total number of digits, however, we show here that the actual number of possibilities is far lesser and technically feasible on fast hardware for even large numbers. Further, the algorithm lends itself to easy parallelization and could be performed on SIMD devices and GPGPUs for much faster solutions.

## Introduction

Consider for the moment a 4 digit number $[a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4]$ and let its factors be represented as two 4 digit numbers represented as $[b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4]$ and $[c_1,c_2,c_3, c_4]$. It is obvious that the factors of a number cannot have more digits than the number so we allow for any of the digits $b_i$ and $c_i$ to be zero to account for it. Now the product can be represented as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_1, b_2, b_3, b_4\\
c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4\\
----\\
b_1 c_4, b_2 c_4, b_3 c_4, b_4c_4\\
b_1c_3, b_2 c_3, b_3 c_3, b_4 c_3, 0\\
b_1c_2, b_2 c_2, b_3 c_2, b_4 c_2, 0, 0\\
b_1 c_1, b_2 c_1, b_3 c_1, b_4 c_1, 0, 0, 0\\
-----------\\
b_1c_1+C_1, b_1 c_2 + b_2 c_1 +C_2, b_1 c_3 + b_2 c_2 + b_3 c_1+C_3, \\
b_1c_4+b_2c_3+b_3c_2+b_4c_1+C_4,b_2c_4+b_3c_3+b_4c_2+C_5,\\
b_3c_4+b_4c_3+C_6, b_4c_4  
\end{aligned}
$$

where the $C_i$s represent the carry digits. ignoring the carry digits we can see that the product can in general be represented as:

$$
\sum_{k=\max(1,i-n+1)}^{\min(i,n)} b_k c_{i-k+1}
$$

because as is clear, the $i^{th}$ term is the term whose indices sum to $i+1$

For simplicity, we can assume that the number $a$ is generated by the product of two $n$ digit numbers $b$ and $c$, with the possibility that even the leading digits (i.e. the most significant digits) can also be zero.

Now we begin making claims about the two digits from the units place. First we generate the whole multiplication table of single digits and notice that every digit in the units place has only a few possibilities. Thus, we can claim that a particular pair of digits satisfies the product constraint for the last digit and work our way forwards towards the next digit. Now, we state empirically that we will have an equation of the form as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
lx + my + C = kp
\end{aligned}
$$

where for example for the second digit we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
l = b_4 \\
x = c_3 \\
m = c_4 \\
y = b_3 \\
C = C_6 \\
p = \textnormal{the unit's digit at the 2nd place} \\
k = \textnormal{the carry term}
\end{aligned}
$$

similarly we have the same equation for the $i^{th}$ term of the expression where the only unknows are the variables $x$, $y$ and $k$ all the other terms are known from the previous claim.

Further, we have, since $l,x,m,y$ are all single digit numbers, it follows that
$$
kp \le C + 162?
$$

since $p$ is known, there is a very strict condition on $k$ and we need only solve an integer programming problem.
