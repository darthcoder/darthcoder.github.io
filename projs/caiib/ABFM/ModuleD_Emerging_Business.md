## Unit 1: Hybrid Finance

### What "Hybrid" Means

```
Hybrid = instrument with BOTH debt AND equity characteristics

Not debt:  may not have guaranteed repayment
Not equity: may have fixed returns, priority on liquidation

Spectrum: Pure Debt ←——————————————→ Pure Equity
             Debentures | FCCBs | Conv. Debentures | Pref Shares | Equity
```

### Preference Shares — The Full Taxonomy

| Type | Key Feature |
|---|---|
| **Cumulative** | Unpaid dividends ACCUMULATE; arrears must be paid before equity |
| **Non-cumulative** | Unpaid dividends lapse (no arrears) |
| **Participating** | Gets extra dividend if profits exceed threshold |
| **Non-participating** | Fixed dividend only |
| **Redeemable** | Must be redeemed within **20 years** (Sec 55, Companies Act) |
| **Irredeemable/Perpetual** | Cannot be redeemed (only non-redeemable pref shares allowed since 1988) |
| **Convertible** | Converts to equity at specified date |
| **Non-convertible** | Stays as preference forever |
| **Adjustable-rate** | Dividend rate varies with market rates |

> **Exam rule:** Redeemable preference shares — maximum tenure = **20 years.**
> Voting rights for preference shareholders trigger after dividend unpaid for **2 consecutive years** (Section 47, Companies Act).

### PNCPS — The Regulatory Instrument

```
PNCPS = Perpetual Non-Cumulative Preference Shares

Issued by: Banks
Capital treatment: Additional Tier 1 (AT1) capital under Basel III
Maximum limit: 1.5% of Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA)

Foreign investment limits:
  FIIs: up to 49%
  NRIs: up to 24%

Key features:
  "Perpetual" = no maturity (doesn't need redemption)
  "Non-cumulative" = missed dividends don't accumulate
  → Absorbs losses while bank remains a going concern
```

> **Exam trap:** PNCPS ≠ ordinary preference shares.
> PNCPS is REGULATORY capital — it absorbs losses when the bank is under stress.
> Bank CAN cancel dividend (no obligation) without creating arrears (non-cumulative).

### Warrants

```
Warrant = RIGHT (not obligation) to buy equity shares at a FIXED PRICE (exercise price)
          within a specified time period

Key features:
- 25% upfront payment required at allotment on preferential basis
- Remaining 75% paid on exercise
- Exercise price typically set 20-30% above current market price at issuance
- Valuation: Black-Scholes model (same as stock options)

Why companies issue warrants:
- Sweetener for debt or other instruments
- Deferred equity — raises capital only when exercised (at higher price)
- Signals management confidence in future performance
```

### Convertible Debentures — The Time Rules

```
Conversion timing rules under SEBI:

< 18 months: Fully Convertible Debentures (FCDs) → AUTOMATIC conversion
             No credit rating required

18–36 months: Optional conversion — holder decides
              Requires credit rating if conversion > 18 months

> 36 months: Requires put/call options embedded in structure
             Credit rating is MANDATORY if > 18 months

Conversion Ratio (CR) = number of equity shares per debenture
Conversion Price (P)  = Par value of debenture / CR
                      = Typically set 20-30% above market price at issuance
```

> **Valuation of optionally convertible debentures:**
> Value ≥ compulsorily convertible debenture value (option to NOT convert has positive value).
> For well-established companies, the option premium is small.

### Mezzanine Finance

```
Mezzanine = between senior debt and equity in the capital structure

Key features:
- Unsecured (subordinated to senior debt)
- Lender gets RIGHT to convert to equity if default
- Higher return than senior debt (compensates for subordination)
- Cost: cheaper than equity but more expensive than senior debt
- Maturity: typically > 5 years
- Use: project finance, acquisitions where pure debt isn't sufficient

Characterisation: "very expensive debt" OR "cheap equity" — both valid
```

### FCCBs — Key Facts

```
FCCB = Foreign Currency Convertible Bond
- Issued OUTSIDE India
- Denominated in FOREIGN CURRENCY
- Hybrid: regular coupon + conversion option to equity
- If stock price < conversion price at maturity → NO conversion → company repays in FX
- FX risk is on the ISSUER
- Typical investors: hedge funds, foreign institutional investors
- Coupon is LOWER than straight bond (conversion option compensates investor)
- Issued in stable, low-interest-rate currencies
```

## Unit 2: Startup Finance

### Startup Definition — Two Thresholds (Exam Loves These)

```
Initial definition (old):
  - ≤ 5 years from incorporation
  - Turnover ≤ Rs 25 crore in any year
  - Working on innovation + technology/IP

Expanded definition (current — under GSR Notification 127(E)):
  - ≤ 10 years from incorporation
  - Turnover ≤ Rs 100 crore in any year
  - Innovation or scalable business model (employment / wealth creation)

In BOTH cases:
  - Must be Pvt Ltd / LLP / Partnership firm
  - NOT formed by splitting or reconstructing an existing business
  - Recognised by DPIIT for benefits
```

> **Exam trap:** "A startup split off from an existing company" → NOT a startup.
> The split-up exclusion is absolute.

### Key Dates (Memorise These)

```
January 2016          → "Startup India" Initiative launched
January 16, 2016      → Startup India Action Plan announced
April 2016            → Startup India Hub launched
2020                  → Incorporation time reduced from 18 days → 4 days; procedures 10 → 3
April 19, 2021        → Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (SISFS) launched by DPIIT
```

### Startup Tax Benefits — The Sections to Know

```
Section 56(2)(viib) (Angel Tax Exemption):
  - Startups exempt from tax on share issuance above FMV
  - Prevents "angel tax" on equity fundraising at premium to FMV

Section 80-IAC (Tax Holiday):
  - 3 consecutive years out of 10 years from incorporation
  - 100% deduction on profits
  - Turnover limit: Rs 100 crore (raised from Rs 25 crore by Finance Act amendment)
  - Effective from April 2021

Section 54GB (Capital Gains Relief):
  - Individual/HUF selling residential property
  - If proceeds invested in eligible startup equity within 1 year
  - → Capital gains exempted

ESOP Taxation:
  - Double taxation: at Exercise (Perquisite = FMV – Exercise Price, TDS deducted)
                 AND at Sale (Capital gains on sale price – FMV at exercise)
  - Post-Budget 2020: TDS on exercise DEFERRED by 5 years (or exit, whichever earlier)
```

### IBC 2016 for Startups

```
Regular companies: wind up in 180 days
Startups ("Fast Track Firms"): wind up in 90 days
- Applies to startups with simple debt structures
- Insolvency professional takes over within 6 months of appointment
```

### Startup Funding Ecosystem

```
Stages:
  Idea → Seed Stage → Series A → Scaling → Exit (IPO/Trade Sale/Secondary)

Types of Funding:
  Debt      = banks, NBFCs, govt loan programmes (requires collateral)
  Equity    = angel, VC, crowdfunding, incubators (no collateral; give ownership)
  Grant     = non-repayable; milestone-based disbursement; Govt/NGO/corporate challenges

Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (SISFS):
  - Launched: April 19, 2021 (announced by PM at Prarambh Summit, Jan 2021)
  - Amount: Rs 945 crore
  - Reaches: ~3,600 entrepreneurs through 300 incubators in 4 years
  - Startup eligibility: DPIIT-recognised, ≤ 2 years old, ≤ Rs 10 million prior funding
  - Incubator eligibility: ≥ 2 years operational, capacity 25 seats, 5 active startups
  - Grant per incubator: up to Rs 5 crore in 3+ instalments
  - Management fee: 0.5% of seed fund award

SIDBI Fund of Funds:
  - Value: Rs 10,000 crore
  - Invests in SEBI-registered AIFs (daughter funds) not directly in startups
  - As of March 2022: Rs 7,225 crore committed to 86 AIFs

Credit Guarantee Scheme:
  - Corpus: Rs 500 crore/year × 4 years = Rs 2,000 crore total
  - Average loan: Rs 5 crore; target: 3,000 startups
```

> **SISFS exam pattern:** Remember: DPIIT launches, 19 April 2021, Rs 945 crore, through 300 incubators.

## Unit 3: Private Equity and Venture Capital

### VC vs PE — The Key Distinction

| Feature | Venture Capital | Private Equity |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage** | Early-stage, startups | Later-stage, established companies |
| **Focus** | Management capability | Corporate governance |
| **Instrument** | Equity, quasi-equity | Equity AND debt (LBO) |
| **Risk** | Very high; 75% fail | Lower; proven businesses |
| **Returns** | 25-35% target | 12-20% |
| **Hold period** | 3-7 years | 5-10 years |

### Why VC Exists

```
Banks require tangible collateral → most startups have none (knowledge economy)
Bank rates would be prohibitively high for startup risk levels

VC fills the gap:
- Takes equity stake instead of debt (no repayment, shares upside)
- Provides strategic guidance + network, not just capital
- Exits via IPO, M&A, or secondary sale after value creation
```

### VC Fund Structure

```
Structure: General Partners (GP) + Limited Partners (LP)

GP: manages the fund; typically owns 1% of fund; full liability
LP: investors (pension funds, insurance, endowments, HNIs, sovereign wealth);
    owns 99%; LIMITED liability

Economics:
  Management fee: 2-3% of fund size annually (GP compensation)
  Carry (carried interest): 20-30% of fund profits to GP
  LP gets: 70-80% of fund profits

Fund life: 7-10 years
  Year 1-3: identify and invest
  Year 3-7: grow portfolio companies
  Year 7-10: exit and return capital
```

### AIF Regulations — SEBI 2012 (Critical for Exam)

```
SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012
Superseded: SEBI (Venture Capital Funds) Regulations, 1996

Category I AIF:
  → Venture capital funds (incl. Angel Funds), SME Funds, Social Venture Funds, Infrastructure Funds
  → Invest in socially/economically desirable areas

Category II AIF:
  → Private equity funds, real estate funds, distressed asset funds
  → No leverage beyond day-to-day operational needs

Category III AIF:
  → Hedge funds, PIPE funds
  → May use leverage, complex strategies, derivatives

Angel Fund (subcategory of Cat I VC):
  → Invests from angel investors
  → Angel investor = individual with ≥ Rs 2 crore net tangible assets (excl. principal residence)
    AND early-stage experience OR serial entrepreneur OR 10+ years senior management
  → OR body corporate with ≥ Rs 10 crore net worth
  → Minimum investment from angel investor: Rs 25 lakh; lock-in: 3 years
```

> **Exam trap:** Category I = VC/angel/social/SME (government-favoured, gets tax incentives).
> Category II = PE, real estate funds (no special benefits; no leverage).
> Category III = hedge funds (can leverage; most complex).

### PE Types

```
Leveraged Buyout (LBO):
  - Most common PE transaction
  - Acquire company using 70-90% DEBT
  - Debt loaded onto acquired company's balance sheet (tax shield benefit)
  - PE firm uses SPV; improves operations; exits via IPO or trade sale

Distressed / Vulture Financing:
  - Invest in struggling companies with underperforming assets
  - Goal: either sell assets at profit OR turn company around

Real Estate PE (REIT):
  - Commercial real estate + investment trusts
  - REITs active in India; minimum investment higher than other PE

Fund of Funds:
  - Invests in OTHER funds (mutual funds, hedge funds)
  - Provides access to funds with high minimums
  - Drawback: higher fees (two layers of management costs)
```

```

- Effective when MANY input variables with NON-LINEAR relationships
- Flaw: "black box" — hard to interpret WHY a decision was made
- Use case: credit scoring, fraud detection, stock prediction

             = sub-field of ML; powers most modern AI applications
```

## Unit 5: Business Analytics

### The Four Types of Analytics — MUST KNOW

```
1. Descriptive Analytics:   What HAPPENED?
   → Interprets historical data; reports; dashboards; KPIs

2. Diagnostic Analytics:    WHY did it happen?
   → Root cause analysis; drill-down; correlation studies

3. Predictive Analytics:    What WILL happen?
   → Statistical models + ML; forecasting; risk scoring

4. Prescriptive Analytics:  What SHOULD we do?
   → Recommends actions based on predicted outcomes
   → Most advanced; feeds into decision support systems
```

> **Exam pattern:** These four always appear in sequence (simplest → most complex).
> Descriptive → Diagnostic → Predictive → Prescriptive.

### Big Data — The 3 Vs

```
Big Data = datasets too large/complex for traditional databases

Characteristics:
  Volume   = amount of data (terabytes to zettabytes)
  Velocity = speed of data generation (real-time streams)
  Variety  = structured + semi-structured + unstructured

Industries using Big Data:
  Manufacturing | Retail | Healthcare | Oil & Gas | Telecom | Financial Services
```

### Elements of Business Analytics

```
Data Mining → Text Mining → Data Aggregation → Forecasting → Data Visualisation
```

## Unit 6: Green and Sustainable Financing

### The Regulatory Framework

```
ISO Technical Committees:
  ISO/TC 207  → Environmental management
  ISO/TC 322  → Sustainable finance
  ISO/TC 309  → Governance of organisations

Key ISO standards for green finance:
  ISO 32210   → Framework for Sustainable Finance
  ISO 14007   → Environmental costs and benefits
  ISO 14008   → Monetary valuation of environmental impacts
  ISO 14097   → Assessing investments related to climate change
  ISO 14000   → Released August 2022; identifying environmental aspects + impacts
```

### Green Bonds — Key Facts

```
Green bond = unsecured debt; proceeds MUST go to "green" projects
Distinguishing feature: issuer's COMMITMENT to use proceeds only for green projects

Green Bond Principles (GBP):
  Established by: International Capital Market Association (ICMA)
  Four components:
    1. Use of proceeds (must qualify as "green")
    2. Project evaluation and selection (criteria + environmental objectives)
    3. Management of proceeds (separate account / sub-account)
    4. Reporting (impact reporting to investors)

Green Masala Bonds: issued OUTSIDE India but denominated in Indian Rupees
                    (foreign investors bear currency risk)

India green bond market: started 2015; outstanding US$16.3 billion as of Feb 2020
                         US$8 billion issued since Jan 2018 = 0.7% of Indian bond market
```

### SEBI Green Bond Categories (9 categories — Exam-Tested)

```
1. Renewable energy (wind, solar, bioenergy, clean technology)
2. Clean transportation (mass/public transit)
3. Sustainable water management (drinking water, recycling)
4. Climate change adaptation
5. Energy efficiency (green buildings)
6. Sustainable waste management (recycling, waste-to-energy)
7. Sustainable land use (forestry, agriculture, afforestation)
8. Biodiversity conservation
9. Any other category specified by SEBI
```

> **Key SEBI rule:** In India, independent reviewer/certifier for green bonds is OPTIONAL
> (international norm = mandatory). Disclosure still required under SEBI ILDS Regulations 2008.

### India Green Finance Policy Timeline

```
Dec 2007   → RBI notification: "CSR, Sustainable Development and Nonfinancial Reporting"
2008       → NAPCC (National Action Plan on Climate Change) developed
2011       → Climate Change Finance Unit (CCFU) established in Ministry of Finance
2012       → SEBI mandates top 100 listed companies (BSE + NSE by market cap) to publish BRRs
2015       → RBI includes small renewable energy under Priority Sector Lending (PSL)
Dec 2015   → SEBI Concept Paper on Green Bonds
May 30, 2017 → SEBI circular on disclosure requirements for Green Debt Securities
2016       → India committed at Paris Agreement (COP21) to reduce emissions intensity 33-35% by 2030
```

### Climate Commitments — COP21 vs COP26

| Target | Paris COP21 (2016) | COP26 (2021) |
|---|---|---|
| Emissions intensity | Reduce 33-35% by 2030 (from 2005 levels) | Reduce carbon intensity < 45% by 2030 |
| Non-fossil electricity | 40% of installed capacity by 2030 | 50% energy from RE by 2030 |
| Renewable capacity | 175 GW by 2022 | 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030 |
| Carbon reduction | — | Reduce 1 billion tons by 2030 |
| Net zero | — | Net zero / carbon neutral by **2070** |

## Unit 7: Special Purpose Acquisition Companies (SPACs)

### What Is a SPAC?

```
SPAC = "blank cheque company"
     = shell company with NO operations
     = raises money via IPO FIRST, then identifies and merges with a target LATER

Also known as: "reverse merger vehicle" or "de-SPAC"

Key structural facts:
  Public shares: 80%  |  Founder shares: 20%
  Trust account: ≥ 100% of IPO proceeds invested in govt securities
  Composition: 95% public investors + 5% sponsors

Timeline:
  18-24 months to find and complete a merger (otherwise: liquidate + return capital)
```

### SPAC vs IPO — Why Companies Choose SPAC

```
SPAC Advantages:
  ✓ Faster (3-6 months vs 12-18 months for IPO)
  ✓ Price certainty upfront (negotiate before merger closes; IPO price depends on market)
  ✓ Can raise additional capital via PIPE (Private Investment in Public Equity)
  ✓ Lower marketing costs (no traditional roadshow)
  ✓ Access to sponsor expertise and network

SPAC Disadvantages:
  ✗ Cannot track where funds go (trust, but limited oversight)
  ✗ Poor post-merger returns historically
  ✗ Shareholder dilution (warrants + founder shares dilute public investors)
  ✗ Increasing regulatory scrutiny
  ✗ Narrow compliance timelines
```

### SPAC Key Terms

```
De-SPAC:        The merger transaction (target merges with SPAC → becomes public)
PIPE:           Private Investment in Public Equity — additional funding alongside De-SPAC
Forward Purchase: Pre-committed equity purchase agreement for De-SPAC transaction

Warrants:
  Sponsor warrants  = net settled (no cash payment; receives shares for net value)
  Public warrants   = cash settled (investor pays cash = strike price; gets shares)
  Strike price      = typically = IPO offer price

De-SPAC Process Steps:
  1. Requirements for Shareholder Approval
  2. Founder Approbation Votes
  3. Disclosure of Material on Super 8-K Form (within 4 business days of closing)
  4. Redemption Offer

Three stakeholder groups: Sponsors | Investors | Targets
```
